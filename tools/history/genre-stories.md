# Genre write-ups

A few sentences about each genre, for its page in Discover and its page for
search engines, under the songs. Where it came from and what to listen for,
written the way the room talks: to you, in short lines, genre names in small
letters. Change any of it: a heading is the genre's name exactly as in
genre-about.csv, each paragraph under it is one paragraph on the page (up to
three, 700 characters each). A genre not here has no write-up. A copy of this
file in history-data/ wins.

## Rock

It starts in the 50s with rock and roll: electric guitars, a hard backbeat and the radio turned all the way up. By the end of the 60s it had split a dozen ways, from hard rock and glam to prog, and punk was right around the corner.

What holds it all together? The band. Guitars up front, bass and drums pushing from behind, and a singer with something to shout about. Turn it up.

## Jazz & Swing

Jazz comes out of New Orleans in the early 1900s, a mix of blues, ragtime and brass bands where the players made it up as they went. In the 30s the big bands of Duke Ellington and Count Basie turned it into swing, and swing was the dance music of its day.

Listen for the walking bass, the brushed snare and the horns answering the soloist. And when those same horns land on a house beat? That's electro swing, and yes, it works.

## Funk

Funk shows up in the mid 60s, when James Brown tells his band to hit the first beat of every bar, "the one", and let everything else dance around it. Sly and the Family Stone and Parliament-Funkadelic took it further out from there.

Forget the melody for a minute. This is about the groove: a bass line that never sits still, a guitar scratching away and horns stabbing in the gaps. Disco, hip hop and house are all built on it.

## Indie Rock

Indie rock starts in the 80s on small labels and college radio, with bands like R.E.M., The Smiths and Pixies who didn't fit the charts and didn't try to. The name was about how the records got made before it was ever about a sound.

The sound it became is guitars with character: jangly, noisy or anthemic, and rarely polished. Then The Strokes and Arctic Monkeys took it right back to the top of the charts in the 00s. It still fills a dancefloor.

## Soul & R&B

Soul comes out of the 50s and 60s, when singers raised in gospel churches brought that fire to rhythm and blues. Motown in Detroit made it pop, Stax in Memphis kept it raw, and Aretha Franklin and Marvin Gaye made it art.

It's the voice. Always the voice, out front, with a warm groove holding it up. Modern R&B keeps the voice and swaps the band for drum machines and deep bass.

## Psych

Psychedelic music blooms in the mid 60s, when The Beatles, Pink Floyd and The 13th Floor Elevators start playing the studio like an instrument: tape run backwards, guitars drowned in fuzz and echo, songs that drift right off the map.

Did it ever really leave? No. Tame Impala and a wave of bands since the late 00s brought the swirl back with modern drums and thick synth, and it sits better on a dancefloor than you'd think.

## Indie Pop

Think of indie pop as indie rock's gentler cousin. It takes shape in mid 80s Britain around the NME's C86 cassette and labels like Sarah Records: jangling guitars, plain voices, big feelings sung quietly.

The hooks are as bright as anything on the radio, only made small and made by hand. Belle and Sebastian set the tone in the 90s, and the bedroom producers have kept it alive ever since. Sing-alongs guaranteed.

## Disco

Disco is born in New York clubs in the early 70s, on dancefloors that were largely Black, Latino and gay, where DJs learned how to keep a room moving all night. By 1977 it was everywhere, from Saturday Night Fever to Donna Summer and Giorgio Moroder's "I Feel Love".

You know the parts: a kick drum on every beat, an open hi-hat between, a bass line that climbs and falls, strings over the top. Nearly everything we dance to now is built from them.

## Dream Pop

Dream pop comes out of early 80s Britain, with Cocteau Twins and the 4AD label, when the guitar stops being a riff and turns into a haze. Shoegaze is its louder sibling.

The voice floats somewhere in the middle of it all, half heard, wrapped in reverb and slow chords like a warm blanket. Beach House brought it to a whole new crowd in the 00s. Close your eyes for this one.

## Post-Punk & Darkwave

Post-punk follows punk in the late 70s, keeping the nerve and dropping the three chords. Joy Division, Siouxsie and the Banshees, The Cure and Bauhaus played it tense and spare, with the bass carrying the tune.

Darkwave is the colder, more electronic side: drum machines, minor-key synths and a voice from the basement. Both came roaring back in the 2010s. And don't let the gloom fool you. This has always been for dancing, lights down.

## Hip Hop

Hip hop begins at block parties in the Bronx in the 70s, when DJs like Kool Herc looped the drum break of a funk record so the dancers could have more of it, and MCs started rhyming over the top.

A beat and a voice. That's still the whole of it, from the sampled breaks of the 80s and 90s to the booming 808s of trap.

## Pop

What is pop, anyway? Whatever the most people are singing along to. It's less a sound than a goal: a chorus that lands the very first time you hear it.

The pop we go for leans toward the dancefloor. Songs big enough for the radio, built on a beat that works in a room.

## New Wave

New wave is pop's answer to punk, from the late 70s into the mid 80s: the same short, sharp songs, played by bands who had found synthesizers and didn't mind a hit. Blondie, Talking Heads, The Cars, Devo and Duran Duran all wore the name.

MTV arrived in 1981 and made it the look of the decade. Those clipped guitars, bright keyboards and deadpan vocals? Indie bands have been borrowing them ever since.

## Indie

Indie is the catch-all: songs from artists doing it their own way that won't sit neatly as rock, pop or dance.

What they share is a point of view more than a sound. Expect guitars next to drum machines, and a singer who sounds like nobody else.

## Chillwave

Chillwave appears in the summer of 2009, named half as a joke by a music blog, for a handful of bedroom producers who happened to sound alike: Washed Out, Toro y Moi and Neon Indian.

It sounds like a memory of 80s pop played off a worn-out cassette. Woozy synth, soft drums, vocals buried in reverb. Not much of a dance, more of a slow sway, and that's the point.

## Indie Dance

This is where the band meets the club. New Order found it in the 80s and Manchester ran with it, but the sound most of us mean was made in the 00s by LCD Soundsystem, The Rapture, Hot Chip and Cut Copy.

Keep the live bass, the cowbell and the singer from a rock band, then set them on a disco or house beat. Songs first, made for dancing. That's pretty much the whole idea of this room.

## Synthpop

Synthpop arrives at the end of the 70s, when synthesizers finally got cheap enough for a band to own. Kraftwerk had shown what the machines could do, and Gary Numan, The Human League, Depeche Mode and Pet Shop Boys turned it into hits.

Pop songs with the guitars swapped for machines: a drum machine, a pulsating bass sequence, a bright lead and a voice that's often cooler than the tune. It comes back every decade, and the new bands sit right next to the old ones. We can't get enough.

## Nu-Disco

Nu-disco is disco rebuilt by people who grew up on house. It takes shape in the 00s, and Norway had a big hand in it: Lindstrøm, Prins Thomas and Todd Terje stretched disco out into long, spacey grooves.

It runs a little slower and warmer than house, with a live-sounding bass, soft pads and plenty of room to move. Edits and remixes are a huge part of it, old records and new songs reworked for the floor. Dig it.

## Downtempo

Downtempo comes out of the chill-out rooms of 90s clubs, where you went to sit down for a minute. Bristol's trip hop, from Massive Attack and Portishead, gave it a darker edge. Air and Thievery Corporation gave it a lounge one.

Slow beats, often borrowed from hip hop, with deep bass and lots of space around them. It's for the end of the night. Or the start of one.

## Electronica

Electronica is the 90s name for electronic music made for listening as much as dancing. Labels like Warp, with Aphex Twin and Boards of Canada, showed that drum machines and samplers could be as personal as a guitar.

The rhythms get busier and the textures get stranger than anything in the club, and somehow there's still a tune you'll be humming on the way home.

## Electronic

Electronic is the wide open space between pop and the club: music made mostly on machines that doesn't belong to any one scene.

A synth instrumental can sit here next to a vocal track or something built entirely from samples. Hard to place? It lands here.

## Deep House

Deep house begins in Chicago in the mid 80s, when Larry Heard, recording as Mr. Fingers, put jazz chords and a soft bass line over a house beat on "Can You Feel It".

It's house with the edges taken off: warm pads, soulful vocals and a kick that rolls more than it pounds. A little slower, a lot smoother.

## Synthwave

Synthwave is a love letter to the 80s that starts in the mid 00s with French producers like Kavinsky and College. Then the film "Drive" came along in 2011 and carried it to everybody.

It pulls influence from the film scores of John Carpenter and Vangelis and from the pop of those same years: gated drums, arpeggiated bass and big analog leads. Made for driving at night, windows down.

## French House

French house, the French touch, comes out of Paris in the mid 90s. Daft Punk's "Homework" in 1997 and Stardust's "Music Sounds Better with You" a year later made it famous, with Cassius and Alan Braxe right behind.

The trick? A disco loop run through a filter that opens and closes, with the whole track ducking under the kick so it seems to breathe. Simple, joyful and still everywhere.

## House

House begins in Chicago in the early 80s and takes its name from the Warehouse, the club where Frankie Knuckles played. The DJs there kept disco alive with drum machines, and local producers started making tracks for them.

The recipe has barely changed: a kick on every beat at around 120 a minute, a clap on the two and four, hi-hats between, and a bass line or piano that lifts. Most of what gets called dance music grew from this.

## UK Garage

UK garage comes out of London in the 90s, when DJs sped up American garage house and pirate radio did the rest. Its best-known form, 2-step, drops some of the kicks so the beat skips instead of marching.

Add chopped, pitched-up vocals and a heavy, rolling bass and you've got it. Artful Dodger and MJ Cole took it into the charts around 2000, and a whole lot of British dance music has come from it since.

## Future Funk

Future funk grows up online in the 2010s, a brighter, faster offshoot of vaporwave. Producers like Macross 82-99, Yung Bae and Night Tempo take Japanese city pop and disco records from the 80s, speed them up and loop the best four bars.

Filter sweeps and pumping compression, borrowed from French house, do the rest. It's nostalgia for a place most of us have never been. Does that make sense? No. We want it anyway.

## Electro

Electro first meant early 80s New York: the 808 drum machine, vocoders and Kraftwerk melodies, the sound of Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" in 1982.

Then in the 00s the name came back, and it came back loud. Justice in Paris, Digitalism in Hamburg and a wave of blog-era producers ran their synths through distortion until dance music hit like a rock band. We play both kinds.

## Breaks & Bass

Breakbeat music is built on a sampled drum break where the steady kick would be. British rave producers in the early 90s sped those breaks up and got jungle and drum and bass, while big beat acts like The Chemical Brothers and Fatboy Slim kept them slower and louder.

Bass music is what came next: dubstep from South London in the early 00s, and everything since that puts the low end first.

## Tech House

Tech house takes shape in mid 90s London, at parties where DJs wanted the swing of house and the drive of techno in the same record.

It's lean and it lives on its drums: a rolling bass line, crisp percussion and not many big melodies. Made for long blends in the middle of a set.

## Techno

Techno comes from Detroit in the mid 80s, where Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson imagined the future with drum machines and synthesizers. Berlin picked it up after the Wall came down and never put it down.

It's machine music in the best sense. Repetitive on purpose, changing a little at a time, until the whole room has moved without noticing.

## EDM

EDM is the festival sound of the 2010s, when dance music filled American stadiums. Swedish House Mafia, Avicii and Skrillex were among its biggest names.

It's all about the build and the drop: tension wound up over sixteen bars, then everything at once. The choruses are meant to be sung by a field full of people.
