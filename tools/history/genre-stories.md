# Genre write-ups

A few sentences about each genre, for its page in Discover and its page for
search engines, under the songs. Where it came from and what to listen for,
in plain words. Change any of it: a heading is the genre's name exactly as in
genre-about.csv, each paragraph under it is one paragraph on the page (up to
three, 700 characters each). A genre not here has no write-up. A copy of this
file in history-data/ wins.

## Rock

Rock grew out of 1950s rock and roll, when electric guitars and a hard backbeat took over the radio. By the end of the 60s it had split a dozen ways: hard rock, glam, prog, and soon punk and the arena anthem.

What holds it together is the band. Guitars up front, bass and drums pushing from behind, and a singer with something to shout about.

## Jazz & Swing

Jazz began in New Orleans in the early 1900s, a mix of blues, ragtime and brass bands where the players made it up as they went. In the 1930s the big bands of Duke Ellington and Count Basie turned it into swing, the dance music of its day.

Listen for the walking bass, the brushed snare and the horn section answering the soloist. Electro swing puts those same horns over a house beat.

## Funk

Funk arrived in the mid-1960s when James Brown told his band to hit the first beat of every bar, "the one", and let everything else dance around it. Sly and the Family Stone and Parliament-Funkadelic took it further out.

The melody matters less than the groove here. A bass line that never sits still, a guitar scratching sixteenth notes and horns stabbing in the gaps: disco, hip hop and house were all built on it.

## Indie Rock

Indie rock started in the 1980s on small labels and college radio, with bands like R.E.M., The Smiths and Pixies who didn't fit the charts and didn't try to. The name meant how the records were made before it meant a sound.

The sound it came to mean is guitars with character: jangly, noisy or anthemic, and rarely polished. The Strokes and Arctic Monkeys carried it back to the top of the charts in the 2000s.

## Soul & R&B

Soul came out of the 1950s and 60s, when singers raised in gospel churches took that fire to rhythm and blues. Motown in Detroit made it pop, Stax in Memphis kept it raw, and Aretha Franklin and Marvin Gaye made it art.

The voice leads and the band holds a warm groove beneath it. Modern R&B keeps the voice and swaps the band for drum machines and deep bass.

## Psych

Psychedelic music bloomed in the mid-1960s, when bands like The Beatles, Pink Floyd and The 13th Floor Elevators began using the studio itself as an instrument: tape run backwards, guitars through fuzz and echo, songs that drift off the map.

It never really left. Tame Impala and a wave of bands since the late 2000s brought the swirl back with modern drums and synths, and it sits surprisingly well on a dance floor.

## Indie Pop

Indie pop is the gentler cousin of indie rock. It took shape in mid-80s Britain around the NME's C86 cassette and labels like Sarah Records: jangling guitars, plain voices, big feelings sung quietly.

The hooks are as bright as anything on the radio, only made small and made by hand. Belle and Sebastian set the tone for the 90s, and bedroom producers have kept it going since.

## Disco

Disco was born in New York clubs in the early 1970s, on dance floors that were largely Black, Latino and gay, where DJs learned to keep a room moving all night. By 1977 it was everywhere, from Saturday Night Fever to Donna Summer and Giorgio Moroder's "I Feel Love".

Its parts are easy to name: a kick drum on every beat, an open hi-hat between, a bass line that climbs and falls, and strings on top. Nearly all dance music since is built from them.

## Dream Pop

Dream pop came out of early-1980s Britain, with Cocteau Twins and the 4AD label, where the guitar stopped being a riff and became a haze. Shoegaze is its louder sibling.

The voice floats in the middle of the mix, half heard, over reverb and slow chords. Beach House brought it to a new audience in the 2000s, and it has shaped a great deal of indie music since.

## Post-Punk & Darkwave

Post-punk followed punk in the late 1970s, keeping the nerve and dropping the three chords. Joy Division, Siouxsie and the Banshees, The Cure and Bauhaus played it tense and spare, with the bass carrying the tune.

Darkwave is its colder, more electronic side: drum machines, minor-key synths and a low voice. Both came back strongly in the 2010s, and both have always been for dancing, lights down.

## Hip Hop

Hip hop began at block parties in the Bronx in the 1970s, when DJs like Kool Herc looped the drum break of a funk record so the dancers could have more of it, and MCs started rhyming over the top.

The beat and the voice are still the whole of it, from the sampled breaks of the 80s and 90s to the booming 808s of trap.

## Pop

Pop is whatever the most people are singing along to, which makes it less a sound than a goal: a chorus that lands the first time you hear it.

The pop played here leans toward the dance floor, with songs big enough for the radio and built on a beat that works in a room.

## New Wave

New wave was pop's answer to punk, from the late 1970s into the mid-80s: the same short, sharp songs, played by bands who had found synthesizers and didn't mind a hit. Blondie, Talking Heads, The Cars, Devo and Duran Duran all wore the name.

MTV arrived in 1981 and made it the look of the decade. The clipped guitars, bright keyboards and deadpan vocals have been borrowed by indie bands ever since.

## Indie

Indie is the catch-all: songs from artists working on their own terms that don't sit neatly as rock, pop or dance.

What they share is a point of view more than a sound. Expect guitars next to drum machines, and a singer who sounds like nobody else.

## Chillwave

Chillwave appeared in the summer of 2009, named half as a joke by a music blog, for a handful of bedroom producers who sounded alike: Washed Out, Toro y Moi and Neon Indian.

It sounds like a memory of 80s pop played off a worn cassette. The synths are woozy, the drums soft and the vocals buried in reverb, and it's all aimed at a slow sway more than a dance.

## Indie Dance

Indie dance is where the band meets the club. New Order found it in the 1980s and Manchester ran with it, but the sound most people mean was made in the 2000s by LCD Soundsystem, The Rapture, Hot Chip and Cut Copy.

It keeps the live bass, the cowbell and the singer from a rock band and sets them on a disco or house beat. They are songs first, made for dancing, which is more or less the idea of this room.

## Synthpop

Synthpop arrived at the end of the 1970s, when synthesizers became cheap enough for a band to own. Kraftwerk had shown what they could do, and Gary Numan, The Human League, Depeche Mode and Pet Shop Boys turned it into hits.

These are pop songs with the guitars swapped for machines: a drum machine, a bass sequence, a bright lead line and a voice that is often cooler than the tune. It has come back in every decade since, and the newer bands sit easily next to the old ones.

## Nu-Disco

Nu-disco is disco rebuilt by people who grew up on house. It took shape in the 2000s, and Norway had a large hand in it: Lindstrøm, Prins Thomas and Todd Terje stretched disco into long, spacey grooves.

It runs a little slower and warmer than house, with a live-sounding bass, soft synth pads and plenty of room. Edits and remixes are a big part of it, with old records and new songs reworked for the floor.

## Downtempo

Downtempo came out of the chill-out rooms of 1990s clubs, where people went to sit down. Bristol's trip hop, from Massive Attack and Portishead, gave it a darker edge, and Air and Thievery Corporation a lounge one.

The beats are slow, often borrowed from hip hop, with deep bass and plenty of space around them. It's for the end of the night, or the start of one.

## Electronica

Electronica is the 1990s name for electronic music made for listening as much as dancing. Labels like Warp, with Aphex Twin and Boards of Canada, showed that drum machines and samplers could be as personal as a guitar.

The rhythms tend to be busier and the sounds stranger than in club music, with a tune you might hum on the way home.

## Electronic

Electronic is the wide field between pop and the club: music made mostly on machines that doesn't belong to one scene.

A synth instrumental can sit here next to a vocal track or something built entirely from samples. If it's hard to place, it lands here.

## Ambient

Brian Eno gave ambient its name in 1978 with "Music for Airports", and its best description: music that is "as ignorable as it is interesting".

There is rarely a beat and often no tune to speak of, only slow-changing tones and texture. It rewards close listening and doesn't demand it.

## Deep House

Deep house began in Chicago in the mid-1980s, when Larry Heard, recording as Mr. Fingers, put jazz chords and a soft bass line over a house beat on "Can You Feel It".

It's house with the edges taken off: warm pads, soulful vocals and a kick that rolls more than it pounds. It tends to sit a little slower, too.

## Synthwave

Synthwave is a love letter to the 1980s that started in the mid-2000s, with French producers like Kavinsky and College. The film "Drive" carried it to a wide audience in 2011.

It borrows from the film scores of John Carpenter and Vangelis and from the pop of the same years: gated drums, arpeggiated bass and big analog leads. It's built for driving at night.

## French House

French house, or the French touch, came out of Paris in the mid-1990s. Daft Punk's "Homework" in 1997 and Stardust's "Music Sounds Better with You" a year later made it famous, with Cassius and Alan Braxe close behind.

The trick is a disco loop run through a filter that opens and closes, with the whole track ducking under the kick drum so it seems to breathe. It's simple, joyful and still everywhere.

## House

House began in Chicago in the early 1980s and takes its name from the Warehouse, the club where Frankie Knuckles played. DJs there kept disco alive with drum machines, and local producers started making tracks for them.

The recipe has barely changed: a kick drum on every beat at around 120 a minute, a clap or snare on the two and four, hi-hats between, and a bass line or piano that lifts. Most of what gets called dance music grew from it.

## UK Garage

UK garage came out of London in the 1990s, when DJs sped up American garage house and pirate radio did the rest. Its best-known form, 2-step, drops some of the kicks so the beat skips instead of marching.

Add chopped, pitched-up vocals and a heavy, rolling bass and you have it. Artful Dodger and MJ Cole took it into the charts around 2000, and a good deal of British dance music since has come from it.

## Future Funk

Future funk grew up online in the 2010s, an upbeat offshoot of vaporwave. Producers like Macross 82-99, Yung Bae and Night Tempo take Japanese city pop and disco records from the 80s, speed them up and loop the best four bars.

Filter sweeps and pumping compression, borrowed from French house, do the rest. It's bright, fast and full of nostalgia for a place most of its fans have never been.

## Electro

Electro first meant early-1980s New York: the Roland TR-808 drum machine, vocoders and Kraftwerk melodies, as on Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" in 1982.

In the 2000s the name came back louder. Justice in Paris, Digitalism in Hamburg and a wave of blog-era producers ran their synths through distortion and made dance music that hit like a rock band. Both kinds are played here.

## Breaks & Bass

Breakbeat music is built on a sampled drum break in place of a steady kick. British rave producers in the early 1990s sped those breaks up and got jungle and drum and bass, while big beat acts like The Chemical Brothers and Fatboy Slim kept them slower and louder.

Bass music is what followed: dubstep from South London in the early 2000s and everything since that puts the low end first.

## Tech House

Tech house took shape in mid-1990s London, at parties where DJs wanted the swing of house and the drive of techno in the same record.

It's lean and built on its drums, with a rolling bass line, crisp percussion and few big melodies. It's made for long blends in the middle of a set.

## Techno

Techno came from Detroit in the mid-1980s, where Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson imagined the future with drum machines and synthesizers. Berlin took it up after the Wall came down and never put it down.

It's machine music in the best sense, repetitive on purpose, changing a little at a time until the room has moved without noticing.

## EDM

EDM is the festival sound of the 2010s, when dance music filled American stadiums. Swedish House Mafia, Avicii and Skrillex were among its biggest names.

It's built around the build and the drop: tension wound up over sixteen bars, then released with everything at once. The choruses are meant to be sung by a field full of people.
